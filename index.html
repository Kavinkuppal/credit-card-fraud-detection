Fraud Detection for Credit Card Transactions

Group 23 | Dr. Mahdi Roozbhani | 2 December 2025

Introduction and Background

Fraudulent activity has become a major issue in areas like credit card transactions and insurance claims, causing significant financial losses for the institutions involved. To address this, researchers have leaned on advanced machine learning and deep learning techniques, developing ways to extract meaningful features and then applying a range of binary classification models. From there, ensemble approaches have emerged as a promising path toward building effective solutions to this challenge.

Literature Review

Fraud detection has seen greater importance as digital transactions expand and fraud rates rise, increasing the need for systems that can accurately identify misuse while remaining efficient [1]. Payment card fraud losses cost “$33.45 billion in 2022 and $33.83 billion in 2023” and “consumers lost nearly $8.8 billion to fraud in 2022” [1].

Several supervised and unsupervised methods have been deemed effective for credit card fraud detection. At the transaction level, when comparing the performance of KNN, SVM, and Artificial Neural Networks in credit card fraud detection using accuracy score, KNN and Artificial Neural Networks were found to have performed better than SVM [2]. Furthermore, when using convolution neural networks for feature extraction, machine learning methods including Naive Bayes, SVM, decision tree, and KNN achieved up to 97% accuracy in fraud detection [3]. It was also found that probability-based similarity measures such as KNN can improve fraud classification while reducing computational costs compared to oversampling techniques and computationally heavy models like XGBoost [1].

Problem Definition

Problem

Fraud is one of the most common challenges in today’s financial environment. It decreases trust, causes heavy economic costs, and creates ripple effects that impact businesses, customers, and society. Because fraudulent activities are often hidden within millions of legitimate transactions, they are difficult to detect. This leaves banks and insurance companies exposed and makes people lose confidence in the system.

Currently, the manual process of auditing and checking for unusual activity to identify fraudulent activities is too time-intensive and can have human errors; there needs to be a more systematic way to identify unusual cases. Because fraud happens so rarely compared to normal transactions, it’s hard for traditional models to catch it without making mistakes. This leaves banks and insurance companies exposed and makes people lose confidence in the system.

Motivation

The motivation for this project comes from the huge impact fraud has on both companies and everyday people. Not only does this increase fees and premiums that honest customers have to pay, outdated systems miss many fraudulent cases or create too many false alarms, frustrating users and wasting resources. By building more reliable fraud detection methods, we can save money, protect consumers, and rebuild trust in financial systems.

Preprocessing

The dataset for this project had 2 CSV files, fraudTrain and fraudTest each with transaction level data as well as labels stating if the transaction was a fraud. Every record had information like transaction date, time, card number, merchant name, merchant category, merchant location, amount, cardholder name, and cardholder address. The target variable, is_fraud, described if the transaction was fraudulent (1) or legitimate (0). Since these raw columns only provided basic details, additional processing and feature engineering was needed to identify behavioral, spatial, and temporal patterns that could identify fraudulent activity. Feature engineering was a major part of this project, because it allowed the model to capture unusualness from normal user behavior like unusually high transaction amounts, abnormal purchase times, or geographically impossible transactions.

First, we focused on time-based features as we could extract them from the preexisting trans_date_trans_time column. We identified the day of week, hour of day, weekend and nighttime variables to help capture transactions that happen at unusual times or days. Next, we engineered amount-based features to describe how each transaction compared to the cardholder’s usual spending behavior. The amt_to_median_ratio feature measured how the current transaction amount compared to the median transaction amount for that cardholder, while the amt_zscore captured how many standard deviations the amount was away from their average spending. These metrics help the model identify transactions that are abnormally large or small compared to each user’s typical spending habits.

We also included merchant-related features to represent a user’s relationship with different stores and categories. The new_merchant_flag feature indicated if a cardholder was shopping at a merchant for the first time, while the category_frequency feature measured how often a user made purchases in a particular merchant category. Fraudulent activity often occurs at new or rarely used merchants, so these features provide important behavioral context.

In addition, we engineered several distance-based features using the latitude and longitude of both the cardholder and merchant. The transDistance feature calculated the distance between the cardholder’s home and the merchant’s location for each transaction. The merchant_travel_distance_24hr feature measured the total distance traveled between consecutive merchants within a 24-hour window for each cardholder. Finally, the impossible_travel_flag identified whether two consecutive transactions implied an impossible travel speed (greater than 300 km/h). These geographic features are critical for detecting cases where a card is used in two far-apart locations in an unrealistically short amount of time.

To capture patterns in transaction frequency, we engineered several velocity-based features. These included the number of transactions made in the past hour (transaction_count_1h) and the past 24 hours (transaction_count_24h), as well as the total money spent in those same windows (total_amount_1h and total_amount_24h). We also created time_since_last_transaction, which measured the elapsed time in seconds since the user’s previous transaction. Fraudulent transactions often occur in rapid succession, so these features help the model recognize bursts of unusual activity.

After all features were engineered, the data was standardized and prepared for model training. Numerical variables were scaled using StandardScaler() to ensure that all features contributed equally to the model, while categorical features such as day_of_week and hour_of_day were one-hot encoded. Finally, because the dataset was imbalanced, with fraudulent transactions representing only a small fraction of all records, we applied the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) to balance the classes and improve model performance on the smaller class.

Methodology

Random Forest: Random Forest was used as our baseline supervised model because it works really well with structured data and doesn’t require any intensive parameter tuning. After preprocessing the data with numerical scaling and one-hot encoding for the categorical fields, we trained the model with the features we had engineered. We used cross-validation to select reasonable values for number of trees as well as tree depth so the model didn’t overfit. We also trained with balanced class weighting to make sure that fraudulent transactions weren’t ignored during the learning. This setup allowed the model to capture any nonlinear patterns while staying efficient to train.

SGD Classifier: SGD was implemented as a fast linear baseline to compare against our other complex models. It’s able to handle large datasets efficiently and update parameters through incremental learning. We used the same preprocessing as before, where numeric features were scaled and categorical variables were one-hot encoded before training. Class balancing was also included to make sure fraud cases had a higher weight during optimization. Since SGD has a linear decision boundary, it doesn’t capture nonlinear interactions in the data, but it did provide us a reference point to see how much the engineering features alone contributed to determining fraud or not.

MLP: MPL was trained to look for more complex relationships between our features than our linear models could. We used the same preprocessing steps of scaling numeric variables and one-hot encoding categorical variables and then trained on the engineered feature set. Class balancing was enabled so training wasn’t dominated by the majority non-fraud class. MLP adjusts internal weights during training to combine features in different ways, so it was able to learn different patterns such as combinations of unusual spending at unfamiliar merchants. This overall gave us a more flexible model that could represent interactions not previously represented.

XGBoost: Several steps were taken in the XGBoost Classifier implementation to improve performance and address a primary concern for the model which is overfitting. As a preprocessing step, StandardScaler was used to handle numeric data while OneHotEncoder was used to handle categorical data. For the following steps, a pipeline and grid search setup was used for hyperparameter tuning. The objective set for the XGBoost Classifier was a “binary:logistic” objective which would help predict fraudulent versus non-fraudulent transactions. For hyperparameter tuning, several combinations of learning rates, tree depths, subsamples, and colsamples by tree were evaluated over 3-fold cross-validation, creating for an appropriate computational time while also improving hyperparameter tuning. The classifications for the test data then occurred on the best combination of parameters based on best training Area Under Precision-Recall Curve score. All of these steps leveraged the XGBoost and scikit-learn libraries.

Results + Discussions + Visualizations

Across all models in this project, overall accuracy tends to be high because the dataset is imbalanced since the majority of transactions are legitimate, and only a very small fraction are fraudulent. As a result, accuracy does not reflect model performance very strongly. Instead, we focus on recall and false negatives, since missing a fraudulent transaction (a false negative) is more costly and risky than incorrectly flagging a legitimate one (a false positive). Our goal is to minimize false negatives while keeping false positives manageable. For recall, precision, and F1-score, we used those associated with fraudulent transactions specifically. We found that across our models, these scores were close to perfect for non-fraudulent transactions, and since our focus has been to minimize undetected fraudulent transactions, these metrics are evaluated accordingly.

Random Forest

Random Forest: Random forest was the first supervised model assessed for fraud classification. We found that random forest achieved a high recall of 0.85 which is strong considering our goal in identifying most fraudulent transactions and minimizing the number of missed frauds. While precision is not as important in our analysis due to our focus on minimizing false negatives, the low precision of 0.22 suggests that some legitimate transactions are incorrectly flagged. This may be acceptable in fraud detection since false positives are less costly than false negatives but could be improved. False negative rate was 15.25% which while low leaves room for improvement since this is the most costly classification for consumers and financial institutions.

Unsupervised Learning

Methodology

To complement the supervised models developed earlier, we incorporated K-Means clusteringas an unsupervised technique to capture behavioral groupings within transaction data. The idea was to find natural clusters among users and transactions that share similar spending patterns, geographic characteristics, or temporal habits but doing this without using the target fraud labels. This allows the model to recognize structure in the data from patterns, something supervised learning classifiers may miss.

After testing values of k between 1 and 20 using the Elbow Method, we observed the “elbow” point at k = 4, indicating four distinct clusters that best balance within-cluster compactness and between-cluster separation Each cluster likely represents a behavioral spending group—such as frequent small transactions, infrequent large purchases, or geographically dispersed activity.

We then assigned each transaction a cluster label based on its K-Means grouping and created one-hot encoded dummy variables cluster_0, cluster_1, cluster_2, cluster_3 to represent these groups. These new features were appended to the training and test datasets. Finally, we retrained our strongest supervised model, XGBoost, on this enhanced dataset to evaluate whether the inclusion of unsupervised cluster features improved fraud detection performance.

Interpretation and Impact

The unsupervised learning component added a valuable behavioral dimension to the fraud detection pipeline. By grouping transactions based on hidden similarities in spending behavior, K-Means clustering captured underlying structurethat supervised models alone could not.

These clusters effectively served as “profiles,” allowing the XGBoost model to contextualize individual transactions. For instance, if a cardholder who typically belongs to a “low-spending, local transactions” cluster suddenly makes a high-value, geographically distant purchase, the model can treat it as suspicious even before seeing enough labeled fraud examples of that pattern.

Future Work

Eventually we want to make our fraud detection system feel closer to how real financial institutions operate. One direction is to tune our models using cost-based thresholds, so that missing a fraud case is treated as much more serious than flagging a few extra normal transactions. We are also interested in adding time-based and sequence-aware models that follow a cardholder’s behavior over days or weeks, rather than looking at each transaction in isolation.
